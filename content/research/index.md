My research asks how the brain's mechanisms can become machine learning. Rather than a chronological paper list, this page groups the work by the questions it tries to answer.

## How does the brain keep time — and can machines learn from it?

The brain tracks intervals and rhythms at every scale, from millisecond spike timing to global oscillations. I study how those mechanisms — especially oscillatory, spike-timing-based computation — can be transposed into learning rules for artificial and spiking neural networks, with an eye toward neuromorphic hardware and edge deployments where energy and online learning matter most.

- [[research/ner-2025/index|SBF-Automata — IEEE NER 2025]] — a reinforcement-learning architecture for periodicity finding at the edge, transposed from the striatal beat-frequency model. *Spotlight poster, IEEE NER 2025.*
- [[research/phd-overview/index|PhD research overview]] — the broader programme: learning rules for deep spiking neural networks in which time dynamics emerge from spike-timing plasticity.
- [[attachments/aamas-2024-oscillatory-timing-models.pdf|Oscillatory Timing Models in RL-Automata (AAMAS 2024, PDF)]] — the precursor poster to the SBF-A line of work.

## How do you choose the right model when inferring networks from data?

Inferring the connectivity of a spiking network from its activity means choosing among models of different complexity. My master's work at the Kavli Institute evaluated a novel information-based Bayesian criterion for exactly this problem in the context of Ising-model inference from neural spiking data.

- [[research/masters-thesis/index|MSc thesis — model selection for Ising inference]] — Kavli Institute, NTNU. *Full web-native restoration arrives in Phase 2.*
