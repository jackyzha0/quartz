In 2017, I attended a conference presentation by Max Tegmark on the next industrial revolution. He talked about where machine intelligence was headed, how it would reshape entire industries, and the kinds of problems it could solve that we had been brute-forcing for decades. I sat in that audience with over ten years of industry experience and a feeling I could not shake: I was watching the future of my field get built, and I was on the wrong side of the glass. I did not just want to use these tools. I wanted to understand them deeply enough to build them for the problems I cared about.

My son had been born that March. I was supporting my wife, adjusting to the sleep deprivation of a newborn, and working full time. It was, by any rational measure, a terrible time to add graduate school to my life.

I enrolled in a master's program in early 2018 anyway. Most of the advice I found online about graduate school was written for 22-year-olds deciding between a PhD and their first real job. Almost none of it spoke to someone in my situation: a young family, a mortgage, a career, a body that no longer runs on four hours of sleep and cold pizza.

So this is the post I wish I had found back then. Not a motivational pep talk. Not a list of reasons you should or should not go back to school. Just an honest account of what it actually looks like to pursue a master's or PhD while working full time, written for people who are close to 40, or past it, and wondering if the window has closed.

It has not.

## Why people our age go back

The reasons are different from what drives a 22-year-old into grad school. At 22, you might be following a default path, or chasing prestige, or genuinely unsure what else to do. At 37, you know exactly why you are there. That clarity is one of the biggest advantages you will have.

For me, the motivation was a specific conviction: the biggest unsolved problems in global access to healthcare will be solved by building intelligent software. I had spent years watching healthcare systems struggle with the same bottlenecks, too few specialists, too many patients, too much variability in diagnosis quality across regions and income levels. I believed that machine learning applied to medical imaging and clinical decision-making could change that equation. Not in some distant theoretical future, but within a timeframe that made it worth retraining for.

That belief is what got me through the master's program in 2018 and 2019. And it is what pulled me back toward a PhD in late 2020, when I realized the master's had given me tools but not the depth I needed to work on the problems I actually cared about. The intellectual itch was unresolved. I could use the techniques, but I did not understand them well enough to push them forward.

From talking to dozens of working professionals in graduate programs, the motivations tend to cluster into a few patterns.

| Motivation | What it looks like in practice |
| --- | --- |
| **Career ceiling** | You have hit a point where the next role requires a credential you do not have. Common in healthcare, data science, and engineering management. |
| **Intellectual itch** | You spent years solving applied problems and now you want to understand the theory underneath. You read papers for fun and started wondering what it would be like to write one. The master's scratched the surface. It did not go deep enough. |
| **Career pivot** | You want to move into research, academia, or a technical specialty that your current experience alone will not get you into. |
| **A moment that rewired your thinking** | You attended a talk, read a paper, or had a conversation that made you see your field differently. For me it was Max Tegmark on stage talking about the next industrial revolution. For you it might be something else entirely. But you know the moment. |

None of these are better or worse than the others. But knowing which one drives you matters, because it shapes everything from which program you pick to how you structure your time.

## The gap between master's and PhD (and why it matters)

I finished my master's in 2019. For about a year, I went back to work and tried to apply what I had learned. It was useful. I was better at my job. But the questions I was most interested in, the ones about how to make medical vision models actually reliable in clinical settings, those required a kind of sustained, deep inquiry that industry work does not support. You get six weeks to build a prototype, not six months to understand why the prototype fails on edge cases.

Then 2020 happened. COVID-19 shut down the world. There was uncertainty at work, uncertainty everywhere. Nobody knew what the next month looked like, let alone the next year. And yet, sitting at home during lockdown, I found myself reading research papers again on evenings and weekends. Maybe it was the forced stillness. Maybe it was watching a pandemic expose exactly the kind of healthcare gaps I had been thinking about. Whatever it was, the itch came back stronger than before.

By late 2020, I started reaching out to researchers whose work overlapped with mine. One of those conversations turned into a relationship with my eventual PhD advisor. The transition from master's to PhD was not a single dramatic decision. It was a slow realization that the itch had not gone away, followed by finding someone willing to help me scratch it.

If you are finishing a master's and feeling that same restlessness, give it some time. If it fades, you probably got what you needed. If it does not, start having conversations with potential advisors. The worst they can say is no.

## The application: what actually matters when you are older

Here is something nobody told me. Being older is not a disadvantage in the application process. In many programs, it is an advantage. Admissions committees, especially at the PhD level, are looking for people who can do independent research. A decade of real-world experience gives you something most 22-year-old applicants cannot demonstrate: evidence that you can manage complex projects, work with ambiguity, and finish what you start.

Your application will look different from a traditional one. That is fine. Here is how I would think about each piece.

**Letters of recommendation.** If you have been out of school for ten or more years, you probably do not have professors who remember you well. That is okay. A strong letter from a manager or technical lead who can speak to your analytical thinking and intellectual curiosity is more valuable than a lukewarm letter from a professor who vaguely recalls your face. If you have collaborated with any academics through work, even informally, those letters carry weight.

**Statement of purpose.** This is where your age becomes a weapon. You can write about specific problems you encountered in industry that drove you toward research. You can point to gaps in the literature that you noticed while doing applied work. Younger applicants often write vague statements about wanting to "advance knowledge in the field." You can write about the exact question that kept you up at night and why existing tools were not sufficient to answer it. Be specific. Name papers. Describe the problem in concrete terms. Show that you have already been thinking like a researcher.

**GRE and standardized tests.** Many programs have dropped the GRE requirement, especially post-2020. If yours still requires it, give yourself enough preparation time. Your test-taking muscles have atrophied. Mine certainly had. I spent six weeks reviewing material I had not touched since undergrad, and it was humbling. But the scores do not need to be perfect. They need to not raise red flags.

**Research experience.** If you do not have any, you can build some before applying. Contribute to an open-source research project. Write a technical blog post that demonstrates analytical thinking. Reach out to a professor whose work interests you and ask if there are collaboration opportunities. I know that sounds intimidating, but faculty are more responsive to working professionals than you might expect, precisely because they know you are serious.

## Picking a program: the constraints are different for us

A 22-year-old might choose a program based on rankings, advisor prestige, or location preferences. You will choose based on a more pragmatic set of constraints.

| Factor | Why it matters for working professionals |
| --- | --- |
| **Flexibility** | Can you take evening or weekend classes? Is hybrid or remote coursework an option? How rigid is the residency requirement? |
| **Advisor fit** | Does your potential advisor have experience with part-time or working students? Some advisors expect 60-hour weeks in the lab. That will not work for you. |
| **Time to degree** | What is the realistic timeline for a part-time student? A PhD that takes 4 years full-time might take 6 to 7 part-time. Make sure you can live with that number. |
| **Funding** | Full-time PhD students often get tuition waivers and stipends. Part-time students often do not. Know the financial picture before you commit. Some employers offer tuition assistance. Check yours. |
| **Cohort culture** | Programs where most students are working professionals feel very different from programs where you are the only one over 30. Both can work, but the social and logistical support differs. |

I want to be direct about something. The "best" program for you is probably not the highest-ranked one. It is the one that will let you actually finish while maintaining your career and your sanity. I have watched people chase prestige into programs that were structurally incompatible with their lives, and most of them either burned out or dropped out.

## The first semester: expect an identity crisis

You will sit in a class with people who were in middle school when you started your career. Some of them will be sharper than you at certain things, especially coursework. They have been in student mode for 16 consecutive years. You have not taken an exam since the early 2010s.

This is disorienting. You are used to being competent. You are used to being the person in the room who knows things. Suddenly you are the person in the room who cannot remember how to take a proper derivative, or who needs three readings to parse a paper that the 24-year-old next to you absorbed in one sitting.

Let me save you some time: this feeling passes. Within a semester or two, the balance shifts. Your ability to connect theoretical concepts to real-world problems, to ask "but does this actually work in practice?", to manage your time across competing priorities, these are skills that younger students are still developing. You bring a contextual richness to discussions that cannot be faked.

The adjustment is real, though. Give yourself permission to be bad at being a student again for a few months.

## Time management: the thing that will make or break you

I will not sugarcoat this. Time is the scarcest resource you have, and the single biggest reason working professionals do not finish graduate degrees is that they underestimate how little of it they have.

Here is roughly what my weekly schedule looks like during the semester.

| Block | Hours | What fills it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full-time job | 40 to 45 | Non-negotiable. This pays the bills. |
| Coursework and studying | 10 to 15 | Readings, problem sets, writing. More during exam weeks. |
| Research | 10 to 15 | Experiments, paper writing, advisor meetings. This is where the PhD actually happens. |
| Sleep | 51 | Six hours on weeknights, nine on Friday through Sunday. The weekend recovery is non-negotiable. |
| Everything else | 42 to 57 | Family, exercise, cooking, commuting, existing as a human being. |

That adds up to a 60 to 75 hour work week during the semester. It is a lot. It is manageable, but only if you are deliberate about how you spend the hours that remain. I still watch TV. I still watch YouTube videos. I did not become a monk. But I got better at noticing when I was scrolling out of habit versus actually recharging, and I learned to protect the recharging and cut the habit. You do not have to give up everything you enjoy. You just have to be honest about what fits.

Three practices that kept me functional.

First, I time-block ruthlessly. Every Sunday evening, I plan the week in 90-minute blocks. Research gets the morning hours on weekends when my brain is freshest. Coursework gets weekday evenings. I do not mix these. Context-switching between job work and academic work is expensive, and minimizing it is worth the rigidity.

Second, I learned to say no. To extra projects at work. To social obligations. To interesting but non-essential academic opportunities. Every yes to something new is a no to sleep, research progress, or the people I care about. Being selective is not being antisocial. It is being realistic about finite hours.

Third, I communicate constantly with my advisor. If a work deadline is going to slow my research for two weeks, I say so immediately. No surprises. Advisors who work with part-time students understand this rhythm, but only if you keep them informed.

## Your employer: ally or obstacle

Your employer's stance toward your degree will shape the entire experience. I got lucky. My bosses at Medtronic have been genuinely supportive, and my colleagues there are some of the best people I have worked with. They understood what I was doing and why, and they never made me feel like I was splitting my loyalty. That kind of environment is not universal, and I do not take it for granted.

A practical detail that might sound small but mattered enormously: I keep two laptops. One for work, one for school. They sit side by side on my desk most evenings. The physical separation sounds trivial, but it enforces a mental boundary. When I close the Medtronic laptop, work is done. When I open the other one, I am a student. No mixing of browser tabs, no accidentally checking work email while reading a paper, no blurred lines about which machine has which files. If your employer has any concerns about IP or resource usage, two separate machines eliminates the ambiguity entirely.

Not everyone will have the kind of workplace support I had. Some employers are neutral. They will not stop you, but they will not accommodate you either. You will need to do your coursework and research entirely on your own time, and you will need to be careful that your academic work does not visibly affect your job performance.

A few are actively hostile. They see graduate school as a sign you are planning to leave, and they are not entirely wrong. If you work in this kind of environment, be strategic about how and when you disclose.

My recommendation: if you can manage the time commitment without any accommodation from your employer, you do not owe them an explanation about how you spend your evenings and weekends. But if you work somewhere supportive, lean into it. Tell your manager. Let your team know. The people around you cannot help if they do not know what you are carrying.

One important note: check your employment contract for intellectual property clauses. Some companies claim ownership of work done on company time or using company resources, and some interpret "company resources" broadly. If your research has any overlap with your job, get clarity on this before you start. The two-laptop approach helps here, but read the fine print regardless.

## The money question

Graduate school is expensive if you are paying out of pocket. A part-time master's at a private university can run $50,000 to $100,000 or more. PhD programs often waive tuition for full-time students but not for part-time ones.

Here is how working professionals typically handle it.

| Funding source | Reality check |
| --- | --- |
| **Employer tuition assistance** | Common for master's degrees, rare for PhDs. Often capped at $5,250 per year (the US tax-free limit). May require you to stay at the company for a period after graduation. |
| **Teaching or research assistantship** | Usually requires a time commitment that is hard to combine with a full-time job. But some programs offer partial assistantships with reduced obligations. Worth asking about. |
| **Self-funding** | The most common path for part-time students. Budget carefully and remember that the degree takes longer part-time, so the total cost is spread over more years. |
| **Scholarships and fellowships** | They exist for non-traditional students, but finding them takes effort. Professional associations in your field are a good place to start. |
| **Loans** | A last resort in my opinion, especially if you already have a mortgage. Taking on significant debt in your late 30s for a degree that may or may not increase your earning power is a risk that deserves careful analysis. |

I want to be honest here. A master's degree in a high-demand field like computer science, data science, or nursing often has a clear return on investment. A PhD is a different calculation. If you are pursuing a PhD for career advancement, make sure the career you are advancing toward actually requires one. If you are pursuing it for intellectual fulfillment, that is a legitimate reason, but go in with your eyes open about the financial cost.

## I failed my first thesis defense

I am including this because nobody talks about it, and I think the silence does real harm.

I failed my first thesis defense. I walked into that room having worked on my research for years, balancing it with a full-time job, sacrificing weekends and evenings, and I did not pass. The committee had concerns about the methodology, about gaps in my argument, about areas where the work needed to go deeper. They were right about most of it, which made it worse.

The drive home was the lowest point of this entire journey. I had told people about the defense. My family knew. My colleagues at work knew. And now I had to tell them it did not go well and that I would need to defend again. At 38, failing an academic milestone feels different than it does at 25. You have less patience for the narrative that failure is just a learning opportunity. It feels like a verdict.

But here is what actually happened. My PhD advisor did not treat the failure as a crisis. He treated it as a revision. He sat with me, went through the committee's feedback point by point, and helped me build a plan to address every concern. That kind of steady, patient support from an advisor is not something you can take for granted, and it made all the difference. I went back and tightened the methodology. I filled the gaps. I read more, ran more experiments, rewrote sections I thought were finished. The second defense went differently. The work was genuinely stronger, not just good enough to pass, but better than what I had originally submitted. The committee saw that.

I have since learned that failing a thesis defense is far more common than anyone admits. Students do not talk about it because it feels shameful. Advisors do not talk about it because the system treats it as an anomaly. But multiple defenses are a normal part of the process for many students, especially those doing work that is ambitious enough to have rough edges.

If you are preparing for a defense and terrified of failing: that fear is reasonable, and failing is survivable. It is painful and slow and humbling. It is also, if you let it be, the thing that forces you to produce your best work.

## Life does not wait for you to finish your degree

This is the part of the post I thought longest about whether to include. I am including it because the sanitized version of this story, the one where you just manage your time well and push through, is not the truth. The truth is that life keeps happening while you are in school, and some of what happens is devastating.

During my PhD, my wife and I lost a pregnancy. If you have been through that, you know there are no words that make it smaller. It is grief that sits in your body. It does not care about your research timeline or your committee's expectations. I stepped away from the work for a while. I had to.

We also lost both of my in-laws during this period. Two people who were part of the fabric of our daily life, gone. Grief does not arrive once and leave. It comes in waves, sometimes in the middle of a workday, sometimes while you are trying to read a paper, sometimes at 3am when the house is quiet.

At work, there were disruptions too. Reorganizations, shifting priorities, the kind of instability that demands more of your attention precisely when you have the least to spare. There were stretches where I was not sure the job would be there next quarter, which adds a particular kind of pressure when that job is also funding your degree and supporting your family.

And then, in 2024, our daughter was born. Joy in the middle of everything. A second child, a new beginning, and also a complete reset of whatever fragile time-management system I had built. Sleep disappeared again. The schedule I had carefully constructed over years dissolved in a week.

But even as I list all of this, I realize the list is incomplete without the other side. Because even though the years were hard, I had the best people around me.

My wife carried more than her share and never once told me to quit. She understood what this meant to me, even during the stretches when I am not sure I fully understood it myself. My son, who grew up watching his dad study at the kitchen table, who learned early that some evenings Papa has homework too. My daughter, who arrived and reminded me what the whole thing was for. My PhD advisor, who gave me room to slow down when life demanded it and pushed me forward when I was ready. My bosses and colleagues at Medtronic, who treated my education as something worth supporting rather than a distraction from my real job.

I did not do this alone. Nobody does. And if you are going to attempt something like this, the single most important factor is not your GPA or your GRE score or your time-management system. It is whether the people closest to you are willing to walk this road with you. Talk to them before you apply. Not to ask permission, but to make sure they understand what you are signing up for together.

I am not sharing all of this for sympathy. I am sharing it because if you are reading this post and thinking "I would do it, but my life is too complicated right now," I want you to know that my life was complicated the entire time. There was never a clean window. There was never a semester where nothing went wrong. The degree happened anyway, not because I powered through everything with grit and determination, but because I had people who held things together when I could not, and because I learned when to slow down, when to ask for extensions, when to tell my advisor that I needed a month, and when to come back.

If you are going through loss, or illness, or family upheaval while in a program: you are not falling behind. You are living a full life and pursuing a degree at the same time, and those two things will sometimes collide in ways that no time-management advice can fix. The only advice that matters in those moments is to be honest with the people around you and to give yourself the same patience you would give a friend.

## The emotional landscape

Imposter syndrome hits differently at 37 than at 22. At 22, you can tell yourself you are still figuring things out. At 37, you feel like you should have figured things out by now. Sitting in a class and not understanding something feels like a failure in a way it did not when you were younger.

You will feel guilty. About time not spent with your kids. About work you could be doing at your job instead of reading papers. About research you should be doing instead of being present for bedtime. I missed things. School events, weekend mornings, the ordinary unhurried time that families are supposed to have together. My son grew from a baby to a school-age kid during this process, and there are stretches of that I experienced only in fragments between deadlines.

The guilt is circular and persistent, and the only way I have found to manage it is to be fully present wherever I am. When I am working, I work. When I am with my family, I put the laptop away. When I am doing research, I do not check work email. Compartmentalization is not just a productivity technique. It is a survival mechanism.

There will be moments when you question the entire enterprise. Probably several times a semester. A bad grade, a rejected paper, a week where every hour feels spoken for and you wonder what possessed you to add a PhD to an already full life. Grief on top of exhaustion on top of a deadline. These moments are normal. They are not signs that you made the wrong choice. They are signs that you are doing something hard while being fully human.

Talk to other graduate students who are also working, especially those with families. They understand the specific texture of this exhaustion in a way that your colleagues at work and your full-time student peers do not. Find them. They are your people.

## What I would tell the version of me sitting in that conference audience

If I could go back to 2017, to the version of me watching Max Tegmark on stage with a newborn at home and a feeling he could not yet name, I would say this.

You are not too old. You are not too busy. Your life is not too complicated. The window did not close. It never does. People finish PhDs in their 40s, their 50s, and beyond. The pace is different, the path is messier, and the sacrifices are more visible than they are for someone with no obligations. But the work is the same. The satisfaction of solving a hard problem, of contributing something new to your field, of earning those three letters after your name, that does not diminish because you started later.

It will be harder than you think. Not intellectually. You can handle the coursework and the research. The hard part is the relentless negotiation between the life you already have and the life you are building. The hard part is Tuesday nights when you are exhausted from work and you have to read three papers before Thursday. The hard part is telling your friends you cannot make it, again, because you have a deadline. The hard part is walking out of a failed thesis defense and deciding to go back in. The hard part is grieving and studying at the same time, and learning that some semesters you carry both.

But you will also discover something unexpected. The work itself, the deep engagement with ideas, the slow process of becoming an expert in something narrow and specific, it fills a need you did not know you had. After a decade of solving other people's problems, you get to ask your own questions. I wanted to ask whether we could build intelligent software that makes quality healthcare accessible regardless of geography or income. That question carried me through a master's, through a PhD, through a failed defense and a successful one, through loss and through joy. My son was a baby when this started. My daughter was born near the end of it. The work grew alongside my family, tangled up in the same years, the same exhaustion, the same kitchen table.

Your question will be different. But if it is real, it will carry you too.

Start with one step. Email a professor whose work interests you. Look up programs. Talk to someone who did it. You do not have to commit to anything today. But if the question keeps coming back, the kind that surfaces during long drives or quiet evenings or while watching someone on stage describe the future you want to help build, pay attention to it. That question is trying to tell you something.

The best time to start was ten years ago. The second best time is now.
